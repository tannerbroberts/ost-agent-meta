**The need (customer's voice):** "My real goal is something like '10,000 daily active users.' Nothing I could do tomorrow moves that number visibly, so I finish a week with no idea whether I got closer or went nowhere. The thing I *can* move — 'landing page dropoff to 5%' — I can't show ladders up to the goal I actually have."

**Why it matters:** The outcome promises a map "from where the product is today to the metric we want to move." That is a route between two states — not-achieved and achieved — and routes have intermediate positions. A tree with a single altitude either states a goal so distant that no evidence bears on it, or a metric so local that moving it proves nothing. The founder named the tradeoff space between the two as *the job itself*: "Building an app that's worthy of 10,000 daily active users is no small feat, and there are ALL sorts of tradeoffs to make between here and there, but that's the job."

**Litmus test (is this an opportunity, not a solution?):** Several distinct directions — a nested sub-outcome layer, explicit contribution estimates linking a local metric to the distant goal, a route/critical-path view over the tree, staged goals that retire as they are met, or leaving altitude implicit and prioritising by judgement. Passes.

**Note for a human:** This touches the shape of the tree itself, not just its contents — the ruleset currently declares exactly one Outcome at the root. Adopting a sub-outcome layer is a structural change and is proposed here, deliberately, rather than assumed.

**Provenance caveat:** Founder-stated in a single spoken rant, not sourced from a story-based customer interview. Believability rests on the floor rung (`assertion`). This is a hypothesis about a need, not an observed need; a human should confirm or discard it against real customer conversations before anything is built off it.
